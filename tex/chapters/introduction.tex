\section{Wastewater}
\subsection{Definition of Wastewater}
Wastewater is broadly defined as any water that has been used and is no longer suitable for its original purpose. It includes water contaminated with pollutants, chemicals, or microorganisms, rendering it unsafe for human consumption or environmental discharge without treatment. As defined by \href{https://www.unescwa.org/about}{UNESCWA}:

\begin{quote}
    \centering
    \enquote{Water which is of no further immediate value to the purpose for which it was used or in the pursuit of which it was produced because of its quality, quantity or time of occurrence. However, wastewater from one user can be a potential supply to another user elsewhere; the spent or used water from homes, communities, farms and businesses that contains enough harmful material to damage the water’s quality.}~\cite{unescwaWastewaterDefinition}
\end{quote}

In an average household, water is supplied from a central source, such as a municipal water supply or a borehole. This water is used for various purposes, including drinking, cooking, bathing, and cleaning. After use, this water becomes \enquote{wastewater}—a term that encompasses all the used water that is no longer suitable for its original purpose.

According to \href{https://bclear-water.com/about-us/}{Bclear Wastewater Solutions}, wastewater can be classified into the following categories \cite{bclearWastewaterTypes}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Domestic (household sewage)
  \item Industrial (from manufacturing and processing)
  \item Agricultural (runoff and process water)
  \item Stormwater (rainwater and surface runoff)
  \item Hospital (clinical effluents)
  \item Mining (effluent from mineral extraction)
  \item Food—processing (wash—water, cleaning effluents)
  \item Construction and demolition
  \item Septic—tank effluent
  \item Grey—water (from baths, sinks, laundry)
  \item Landfill leachate
\end{itemize}

The problem on the treatment of wastewater is vast since there are different ways of handling the different types of wastewater that we have. For example, if we are to take wastewater coming from you kitchen sink, it can be safely recycled into your backyard and will do a marvelous job of watering your plants, and at the same time, add more nutrients to them.

On the other hand, if we are to take a milk production \& processing company, their wastewater will be so full of organic matter from the through cleaning they need to achieve in all the animal sheds and the milk processing that their wastewater will have very high BOD values and will need to be treated in a different way.

Luckily for us, nature has had more time to know how to process energy and convert it into other forms and since we are smart, we have harnessed that power and used it for other applications such as treating our wastewater. 

The FAO talks about the usage of \enquote{water of marginal quality}\cite{fao_wastewater} as an option of lower resort from the lack of clean and safe water for human comsumption but I hope that someday, our water standards will reach a point where we will be able to start recycling our wastewater and not just dumping it and hoping that the hydrological cycle will miraculously \enquote{refill our water supplies}

\subsection{Properties of Wastewater}
Municipal wastewater is predominantly water. Approximately 99.9\% of its volume is water, while the remaining 0.1\% consists of dissolved and suspended solids—encompassing both organic and inorganic materials as well as microorganisms—according to FAO and Wikipedia \cite{fao_wastewater,sewage_wikipedia}.

Wastewater characteristics can be broadly classified into three main groups:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Physical properties
  \item Chemical properties
  \item Biological properties
\end{enumerate}

These three categories are essential for understanding wastewater composition and treatment processes \cite{scienceinfo_wastewater}.
